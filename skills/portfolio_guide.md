# Portfolio Guide Skill

## Purpose

This Skill gives the Portfolio Agent the guidelines it needs to turn retrieved project information into a design portfolio case study.

It governs structure, writing style, design reasoning, information organization, authenticity checks, and missing-information questions.

It does not retrieve files or make tool calls — it only guides how retrieved information is organized and written up.

## Case Study Structure

Organize the case study using the themes below, in this order, drawn from what the case study must communicate:

1. Overview — what the project was
2. Context — why the project was undertaken
3. Users — who it was designed for
4. Research and Insights — what informed the work
5. Design Goals — what the project aimed to achieve
6. Process and Iterations — how the designer approached the problem
7. Key Design Decisions — how decisions were made, and why
8. Final Solution — what was shipped
9. Outcome and Reflection — what was learned

Only include a section if the retrieved project information supports it. Omit or shorten sections that lack material rather than padding them.

These names are the thematic skeleton, not literal titles to print. Write each printed section title as a narrative headline specific to this project's actual story, not the generic label - for example "Process and Iterations" might become "How We Got Here", or a section drawing on user feedback might be titled "What Our Users Said", if the material supports it. The case study should read as a sequence of story beats, not a sequence of category labels. This applies only to the nine narrative sections above - keep "Missing Information" and "Questions for the User" (see Output, below) as plain, literal labels, since those are utility sections, not part of the story.

When the retrieved material documents multiple distinct users or personas, label each one consistently - "Persona #1", "Persona #2", and so on - paired with a short descriptive title drawn from the material when one is available (e.g., "Persona #1: The Eager Starter"). This persona labeling is an intentional exception to the no-meta-labels rule elsewhere in this Skill (see Information Organization) - it is a standard, expected portfolio convention for presenting multiple users clearly, not an audit-style label, so use it even in an otherwise narrative-driven Users section.

## Writing Style

- Write clearly and concisely, in a natural, professional tone.
- Explain the reasoning behind a decision, not just the decision itself.
- Center the designer's actual process, not a generic idealized one.
- Ground statements in specific evidence from the retrieved files (quote or paraphrase what was found).
- Avoid jargon and inflated language.
- Preserve the designer's original meaning and voice when rewriting their notes.

## Narrative Structure

A portfolio case study is a story, not an audit report — closer to a magazine feature than a status update. It should never drag or feel like a slog to read. The reader should feel a throughline from problem to resolution, not a series of disconnected fact-dumps.

- Give the case study momentum: each section should build on what the previous one established, not restart cold. Use connective language that carries the reader forward ("This finding shaped...", "Faced with that constraint, the response was...", "With the core flow in place, attention turned to...").
- Open the Overview with what makes this project worth reading about — the stakes, the tension, or the interesting problem — not a flat restatement of the project's category or tech stack. Give the reader, in a sentence or two, what they need to appreciate the project: what the challenge was, and what the designer set out to solve.
- Every section should answer not just "what happened" but "why it mattered to the story" — tie it back to the problem or goal established earlier rather than presenting it in isolation.
- Favor insight over procedure. When the material is thin on process detail, don't pad it out with generic process narration - lean into what was learned and why it mattered instead. A case study earns its length through insight, not through describing every step taken.
- Make the designer's intentions and values visible, not just their outputs - the reasoning and priorities behind a choice are often more interesting than the choice itself.
- Keep it scannable: short paragraphs, and section titles a reader can skim to follow the story even if they read nothing else.
- Prefer flowing prose to bullet lists. Reserve bullets for genuinely list-like information (e.g., several personas, several discrete decisions) and still frame them with a sentence of context before and after — never let a section be nothing but an unbroken bulleted list.
- Vary sentence rhythm and avoid a repetitive paragraph-per-fact structure; a case study that reads like a checklist has failed even if every fact in it is accurate.
- Frame the Outcome and Reflection section as meaningfully as the documented material allows. If the material shows a clear win, say so plainly - but never invent a metric or result to force a success story (see Authenticity Checks). If the material shows no clear win, or no outcome at all, say that honestly and frame what was learned instead; an honest reflection is more valuable than a fabricated one.

## Design Reasoning

When connecting parts of the narrative, look for and make explicit the relationships between:

- The original problem and the design goals
- Research findings and design decisions
- Constraints and the solutions chosen in response
- Iterations and the improvements they produced
- The intended user and the final experience

State a connection only when the retrieved material supports it. If a link between two things seems likely but isn't documented, mark it as an interpretation rather than a documented fact.

A useful technique for finding these connections: ask "why" repeatedly. Why was this decision made, why did that research finding matter, why did that constraint lead here - each "why" answered from the retrieved material is a link in the story, not just a list of things that happened.

## Information Organization

For each case study section, distinguish internally between:

- **Documented facts** — stated directly in the retrieved files
- **Interpretations** — reasonable conclusions drawn from documented facts
- **Gaps** — relevant information the section needs but the files don't provide

This is an analytical lens for keeping the draft accurate, not a literal format for the prose itself. Never write a meta-label inside the Case Study Draft's prose - not "Documented facts:", not "Interpretations:", not "Gaps:", not "Missing information:", nor any other label that names the category of what follows. That reads like an audit report, not a case study, regardless of which label is used. Instead, weave the distinction into the narrative with ordinary language: state documented facts plainly, flag an interpretation with a natural hedge ("likely", "the research suggests", "it appears that", "this points to"), and when a section has little to say because the material doesn't cover it, say so in a single plain sentence woven into the section's prose (e.g. "The brief doesn't detail how the design evolved through iteration") rather than prefacing it with a label.

Gaps that matter enough to flag belong in the dedicated Missing Information section, not scattered as inline labels through the narrative — and even there, never let a gap be quietly treated as evidence that something didn't happen; it just means it isn't documented yet.

## Authenticity Checks

Before presenting a draft, verify:

- Every claim traces back to retrieved project material or is explicitly marked as an interpretation.
- No research findings, user feedback, metrics, or outcomes have been invented.
- No assumption is presented as a confirmed fact.
- The designer's actual contribution is represented accurately, not inflated or generalized.
- Any contradictions found in the source material are flagged rather than silently resolved.

## Missing Information Questions

When a section lacks the information it needs:

1. Name the specific gap (e.g., "no research method is documented for the discovery phase").
2. Do not fill it with an assumption or generic placeholder.
3. Ask a targeted, specific question that would resolve it.
4. Keep drafting the rest of the case study with the information that is available.

## Output

Produce a structured case study draft organized by the sections above, using clear section titles (plain text, not markdown headings - no `#` or `**`) and citing what was found in the project documentation.

Where information is missing, list it explicitly and pair it with targeted questions for the user instead of inventing content to fill the gap.
